The card that pays you most is not always the one that leaves you the most.

CardRight tells you which card to use for every purchase by counting what your cards cost you, not just what they pay you. It runs on real cards and real store rules, replays your last year, shows where money leaked, and ranks the moves worth making.

The problem

People carry several cards and guess at every checkout.

Each card has its own rules: bonus categories, spending caps, quarterly activations, annual fees, foreign fees, statement credits and protections that nobody remembers. So most people pick a favorite card and use it for everything.

Apps already exist to help. CardPointers, MaxRewards and Kudos tell you which card earns the most points at a store. They are useful, and most are built around one question: which card pays the most?

That is the wrong question for the 47% of cardholders who carry a balance. The regulator has said so:
The insight

Optimize for money kept, not points earned.

CardRight scores every way to pay on one number: what you actually keep.

Money kept = rewards + statement credits + card offers + protections + sign-up bonus progress + tax saved minus interest, foreign fees and processing feesEvery term is shown to the person, with its assumption. Estimates such as the value of an extended warranty are labelled as estimates.

How it works

AI reads the messy parts. Fixed math makes the call.

Real cards make this concrete. Two of the cards in the demo changed their terms in the four months before I built it, and store rules like Costco's Visa-only policy decide which cards even work. The two jobs that need judgment, reading card terms and recognizing merchants, are AI-shaped. The decision itself is plain, tested arithmetic, so the same purchase always gets the same answer and every answer can be checked.

Recognizing merchants now runs on a real model (gpt-oss-120b on Groq), through the same small server and key as my Umbra product. The model only names a category from CardRight's fixed list; it never sees a card or a balance and never picks a card. If it is down, CardRight uses its own name rules and says so. Reading card terms is still a transparent rule-based stand-in, and a person would approve every new card's rules before they go live.

Real scenarios

Real cards at real stores, with the rules that trip people up.

Maya · Costco run

Three of her four cards are refused

Costco warehouses take only Visa credit cards. Her American Express Gold, Freedom Flex and Double Cash are all out, and the app says why before she reaches the register. Her Sapphire Preferred is a Visa, and it is also $380 from a 75,000 point sign-up bonus.

Jordan · Groceries

It steers him away from his favorite card

He carries $3,400 on his Discover at 26.49%, so a $55 grocery run on it costs more in interest than any reward pays. CardRight says use the 2% Active Cash and pay it in full, in one sentence.

Jordan · Replay

A year of spending, rerun

398 purchases replayed. Most of what he lost was interest, not rewards. The line shows his "kept" going below zero while the right cards stay positive.

Theo · Dentist bill

The best card for a crown is not a credit card

His HSA debit card spends pre-tax money. At a 24% tax rate that beats every rewards card he owns by a wide margin, and CardRight counts it in the same currency: dollars kept.

Theo · Quarterly taxes

Paying the IRS by card for the points loses money

The IRS-approved processor charges 1.75% for credit cards. His everyday card earns 1.5%. On a $3,000 estimated payment that is a loss, so the answer is a free bank transfer.

Maya · Plan

A short list, ranked by dollars

Finish the sign-up bonus, route spending by category, turn on the quarterly 5%, and a fee check that is honest both ways: her $325 Gold card only just earns its fee.

The AI steps, on data they were never tuned on

StepTuned setHeld-out setHeld-out misses flaggedMisses not flagged

The rules alone were the weak link: only about half of unfamiliar names landed in the right category, though every miss came back marked "not sure", so the app asked instead of guessing. The model, run on the same 18 held-out names, got 17 right. Its one miss, Midstate Gas Company, it called a gas station and marked sure; it is a utility bill. That is the dangerous kind of mistake in money, so the app keeps the name rules as a second opinion: when they are confident and disagree with the model, it asks the person. With both together: 17 of 18 right, and no silent mistakes. Answers came back in about a third of a second.

Guardrails

Built to be trusted with money decisions.

01

No card numbers, no bank login

The engine needs which cards you hold and a few facts about them. Linking accounts would sharpen it, but it is optional.

02

No paid placement

The "next card" check runs market cards against your real spending and can, and usually does, say no new card is worth it.

03

Every number shows its math and its source

Rewards, interest, fees, tax and estimates are separate lines. Every card carries the date it was checked and the pages it was checked against. What is assumed is listed as assumed.

04

AI assists, rules decide

Reading terms and recognizing merchants can be wrong, so both report confidence and flag doubt. The recommendation is deterministic.

05

Guidance, not advice

It explains trade-offs with the math shown, like which balance costs most. It does not tell anyone how to invest or which loan to take.

If this were a real product

How it makes money without selling cards.

Options I would test

A small subscription, priced against what the replay shows a person leaves on the table. Or licensed to banks and credit unions as a feature inside their own app, where helping customers use the bank's cards well improves retention.

What I would measure

Dollars kept per user against their own replay baseline. Share of checkouts where the recommended card was used. Interest avoided for people who carry balances. How often the plan's top move gets done within 30 days.

Who it is for first

People with three or more cards, and especially people who carry a balance. They have the most to gain and are the least served by points-first tools.

What would stop a launch

Any recommendation that sends new spending to a card with a carried balance. Any stale card term older than its review window. Any sign that a paid relationship influenced a result.

Next

What I would build next.

  • Move the fine print reader onto the model too, constrained to the same output shape, and rerun its held-out set.
  • A card-terms pipeline with a review date on every rule. Two of the cards here changed in the four months before I built this, so stale terms are the main product risk.
  • Statement-date planning: which card to use this week to keep each card under 30% of its limit when it reports.
  • Interviews with people who carry balances, to test whether "stop using this card" lands as help or as judgment.
